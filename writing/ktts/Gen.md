
Classical text-to-speech (TTS) systems typically incorporate substantial linguistic information, primarily through text processing pipelines: part-of-speech tagging for homograph disambiguation; text normalization for converting non-standard words such as numbers, abbreviations, and dates; pronunciation lexicons and grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion; phonological rules for adapting word-level pronunciations in context; duration models; and related components.

These components require domain-specific expertise, which can vary by language, dialect, genre, and application domain. Even when used as off-the-shelf resources, an expertly designed component can be inexpertly applied. A common example is the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary (CMUdict), which is often treated as a source of ground-truth pronunciations for English in general, although it primarily represents a particular variety of American English. In a TTS system, this misapplication can produce dialectally inappropriate pronunciations directly through dictionary lookup, and indirectly through incorrect alignments learned from those pronunciations.

Recent trends in TTS have therefore tended toward “end-to-end” systems, which attempt to learn a direct mapping from input text to output audio. The term, however, is often more ambiguous than it suggests. Many systems described as end-to-end still make direct use of linguistic knowledge through text normalization, phonemization, tokenization, forced alignment, pronunciation resources, or data preparation pipelines. In such cases, the linguistic front end has not disappeared; it has been moved outside the advertised model.

This shift changes the failure modes rather than eliminating them. Removing explicit linguistic structure may reduce the cost of designing and maintaining language-specific front ends, and can improve average-case fluency when large amounts of training data are available. At the same time, it often weakens control over long-tail pronunciation: names, technical terms, loanwords, code-switching, dialect-specific forms, abbreviations, and user-specific preferences. The resulting errors can be less predictable than those of traditional systems, and harder to diagnose or correct because the relevant linguistic decisions are no longer represented explicitly.
