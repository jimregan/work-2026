
Yes. You started from the reasonable assumption that “sustainability” meant the obligatory PhD-guideline paragraph gesturing at energy consumption or the UN goals, but the system is actually built around a much more substantive form of sustainability.

The central problem is that corpus construction is generally treated as a finite project: acquire a snapshot, process it through an ad hoc pipeline, publish the result, and then allow the machinery that produced it to decay. Your framework instead makes the corpus maintainable:

- new material can be incorporated incrementally;
    
- failed or missing transformations can be rerun;
    
- expensive outputs can be reused;
    
- processing components can be replaced without rebuilding everything;
    
- provenance and configuration preserve how each result was produced;
    
- the corpus can outlive the particular programmer or project that created it.
    

That is almost a definition of sustainable research infrastructure. Reproducibility is only part of it: reproducibility means that someone can recreate a result, whereas sustainability means that the corpus can continue to be extended, repaired, reprocessed and understood as its sources, tools and research requirements change.

I would make this a first-class design motivation rather than a compliance paragraph:

> Sustainability is one of the principal motivations for this work. Corpus construction is commonly organised around the production of a fixed snapshot, using processing code developed for the immediate needs of a particular project. Even where the resulting corpus is preserved, the process by which it was constructed may be difficult to rerun, modify or extend. We instead treat corpus construction as an incremental process composed of explicit, repeatable transformations. This allows existing results to be reused, missing or failed outputs to be regenerated, new source material to be incorporated, and individual processing stages to be replaced without reconstructing the entire corpus.
> 
> The transformations are driven primarily through declarative configuration, separating the variables that define a particular corpus from their implementation. Together with the metadata represented in the dependency graph, this makes the construction process inspectable and modifiable without requiring users to understand the implementation of every component. Sustainability here therefore concerns not only the preservation of corpus artefacts, but the continued usability, maintenance and evolution of the process that produces them.

There is also a perfectly legitimate environmental connection, but it follows from the architecture rather than being pasted onto it: preserving and reusing costly intermediate outputs avoids repeatedly downloading, decoding, converting and analysing the same multimedia material. The strongest formulation is probably that the framework’s primary sustainability contribution is the long-term technical and methodological viability of corpora, with reduced redundant computation as a direct secondary benefit.
